🎲 ML Lecture 2 · Is Learning Feasible?

Deep Explanation

Tap blocks to expand. Highlighted = exam favourites.

1 · Recap & the problem

Three criteria to check for ML: pattern exists, can’t pin it down mathematically, have data. Only one is essential:

  • No pattern → you can still try; you’ll fail, but learning theory gives something you can measure that tells you whether you learned. No harm done.
  • Can be programmed mathematically → ML still works but isn’t recommended (not optimal).
  • No data → nothing can be done.

Supervised learning: unknown target f (X→Y), training examples give the outputs explicitly. Unsupervised = no output; reinforcement = only a reward/punishment. Learning model = algorithm + hypothesis set H; g ∈ H hopefully ≈ f. Unknown f is a generous assumption: you needn’t worry about what generated the examples.

The problem (end of lecture 1): I give you f on a finite set. Outside it f can take any value (even on the millionth-and-first point). So how can learning be feasible? This lecture: feasible in a probabilistic sense.

Roadmap: simple probabilistic situation → translate to learning → make it real learning → a dilemma → solution.

2 · The bin experiment
Bin of red/green marbles. μ = probability of red (bin fraction), unknown constant (bin is opaque), P(green)=1−μ. Pick a sample of N marbles independently (infinite bin, or with replacement). ν = fraction of red in the sample (random; changes sample to sample).

Does ν tell us anything about μ?
No (possible): bin 90% red, sample of 100 all green could happen. Yes (probable): if the sample is big enough, ν is likely close to μ. Analogy: presidential poll — 3,000 people predict ~100 million voters. In science/engineering, “almost certain” opens a world of possibilities.

3 · Hoeffding’s Inequality (the key formula)
P[ |ν − μ| > ε ] ≤ 2·e−2ε²N   for any ε>0 and any N≥1
  • Bad event = ν and μ differ by more than tolerance ε. The inequality bounds its probability.
  • Good news: negative exponential in N → bigger sample ⇒ tiny probability.
  • Bad news: exponent has ε². ε=0.1 → 0.01 (weak damping). ε=10⁻⁶ → 10⁻¹², kills any N: bound ≈ 1 (trivial).
  • The factor 2 makes it true (the lecturer prefers it without!).
  • Valid for every N and ε (not asymptotic). One of the laws of large numbers.
  • Uniform bound: right side has no μ although the left probability depends on μ (ν is binomial). So μ unknown is fine.
  • Trade-off N vs ε: smaller ε (stricter) needs bigger N for same bound. N usually dictated; ε is your taste.
  • PAC: μ=ν is Probably (probability of violation small) Approximately (within ε) Correct.
  • Logic: cause is μ → effect ν, but we infer μ from ν. Fine because |ν−μ|>ε is symmetric; only ν is random, μ is a constant.

Hoeffding is the building block for the VC dimension later.

4 · From bin to learning

Mapping: bin ↔ input space X; marble ↔ point x; green = hypothesis got it right, red = wrong. Colours depend on h and f; f stays unknown, so μ unknown.

Addition to the learning diagram: a probability distribution P over X generating x₁…x_N independently.
  • No assumption on P (any P), and P may be unknown (Hoeffding bounds independent of μ).
  • No assumption on f; promise kept.
  • Valid only to the extent the independence/same-P assumption holds.
  • P is on X, not on H (H stays a fixed set; a Bayesian view puts a prior on H, discussed at the end of the course).
5 · Verification ≠ Learning

With one fixed h (e.g. the lecturer improvises weights: salary 2, debt −0.5, years 0.1, threshold 0.5) you can verify how good it is: Hoeffding says E_in(h)≈E_out(h). But you have no control over whether E_in is small; it may be terrible. That is a hypothesis set with one hypothesis.

Real learning: search many hypotheses, pick the one with the smallest E_in ⇒ multiple bins h₁…h_M (finite M for now). Different h ⇒ different colouring of the same points; samples differ. Learning = scan the samples for a good (mostly green) one and declare its bin good.

Notation: ν → E_in(h) (in-sample error), μ → E_out(h) (out-of-sample error; what matters). Both depend on h.
Hoeffding: P[ |E_in(h) − E_out(h)| > ε ] ≤ 2e−2ε²N
6 · The dilemma: why plain Hoeffding fails for many bins

Coin experiment: flip a coin 5 times; some people get 5 heads — not because the coin is biased. For a fair coin P(10 heads)=½¹⁰≈1/1000. But with 1,000 fair coins, P(some coin shows 10 heads) ≈ 62%, “more likely than not”. “You tried too hard.”

Translation: all bins are half red/half green (μ=½, useless hypotheses). Hunting for an all-green sample will find one; reporting it is a mistake. Hoeffding’s guarantee gets diluted by the search.

Solution (modified Hoeffding):
P[ |E_in(g) − E_out(g)| > ε ] ≤ 2M·e−2ε²N
  • Union bound: P(A or B or …) ≤ ΣP. Loose (ignores overlap), worst case assumption: events disjoint. Makes no assumptions ⇒ widest applicability.
  • Each hypothesis is fixed in advance ⇒ plain Hoeffding applies term by term.
  • Price: factor M. 10 hypotheses fine; a million and the bound can exceed 1 (“less than 10: thanks, we needed a graduate course”).
  • Intuition: more complex model (large M) ⇒ E_in tracks E_out more loosely ⇒ memorization, bad generalization (matches lecture 1’s Q&A).
  • Perceptron and nearly all real models have M = ∞ (weights vary continuously) ⇒ bound useless. Next step: replace M by a finite abstract quantity (VC dimension, theory of generalization).

Verdict: learning is feasible in a probabilistic sense: with enough data relative to hypothesis-set size, E_in(g) ≈ E_out(g).

7 · Q&A highlights
  • Bound > 1 (trivial)? Not enough data for that tolerance (equivalent: tolerance too strict). Like polling 5 people.
  • M=∞ for perceptron? Yes; handled in a later lecture.
  • h vs model: h = one function; g = the h you finally pick; model = set H of allowed hypotheses. #hypotheses as complexity is only intuitive; structure of individual hypotheses matters too (later).
  • How to pick g? Depends on algorithm, model and data (e.g. PLA).
  • Real-valued outputs: use expected value vs sample average (error frequency is the 0/1 special case); variance of the variable affects the bound.
  • Why multiple bins? One bin = a hypothesis set with one hypothesis = verification only. Colours change with h.
  • P on X, not H.
  • Many zero-E_in hypotheses? Same M and E_in ⇒ same bound; the algorithm picks one; SVM later prefers one with better generalization chance.
  • Does it hold for any g? Yes: g is whatever the whole learning process outputs; union bound covers all h, including ones visited on the way (overkill but valid).
  • “Does Hoeffding show learning is feasible?” Plain version shows verification; the modified (with M) deals with learning.
  • Bin = general population (before colouring); sample = people polled.
  • Related to p-values/laws of large numbers; the course borrows only this formula.

Smart Summary

  • Problem: f unknown outside the sample ⇒ certainty impossible, probability possible.
  • Bin: μ unknown, ν sample fraction, N independent picks.
    • Hoeffding: P[|ν−μ|>ε] ≤ 2e−2ε²N, all N, ε; independent of μ; PAC.
    • Trade-off: small ε ⇒ large N.
  • Mapping: marble=x, green = h(x)=f(x), red = disagree; add P on X (any, unknown, independent draws).
  • Notation: E_in(h)=ν, E_out(h)=μ.
  • Verification = one fixed h. Learning = choose among M.
  • Coins: 1000 fair coins ⇒ ~62% chance some has 10 heads: searching inflates luck.
  • Union bound ⇒ P[|E_in(g)−E_out(g)|>ε] ≤ 2M e−2ε²N.
  • M large (or ∞) ⇒ bound useless ⇒ next: VC dimension.

Mnemonics

“Two-M-e”: bound = 2·M·e^(−2ε²N): Two, M, e-to-the-minus.

μ = “Mystery” (out/bin), ν = “Visible” (in/sample): ν looks like “v” for visible sample.

Green = Good agreement, Red = Rong disagreement.

PAC: Probably (small probability) · Approximately (within ε) · Correct.

⚠ Traps

  • ν=μ is not claimed; only close with high probability.
  • Hoeffding is not asymptotic; holds for every N, ε.
  • Forgetting the 2 or the ε².
  • Applying plain Hoeffding to the chosen g ✗ (needs the M factor).
  • Saying P must be known or f must be assumed ✗.
  • Putting probability on H ✗ (it’s on X).
  • Thinking union bound is tight ✗ (worst case, disjoint events).
  • Believing 10 heads from a coin proves bias ✗ (1000 coins).
  • Thinking cause/effect reverses the maths: logic only, symmetric inequality.
