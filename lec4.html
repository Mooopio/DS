4. How we set up learning

  • Population: all the data. If we had all of it we could just query it (SQL: SELECT … FROM … WHERE …). We usually don’t.
  • Sample: the part we have. We want to infer something about the population from it.
  • Training examples: inputs paired with outputs (x reflects into y, plus a bias).
  • Hypothesis set H: all candidate lines. We pick one h from H. The unknown target f is approximated by the best candidate, called g.
  • Learning algorithm (here PLA) picks h using the data.
Choosing a different h changes how a new point will be classified. That is why we ask whether learning is feasible.

5. Is learning feasible?

Like a feasibility study for a project, we ask whether learning from a sample is worth it and not an oversimplification. We answer with probability.

6. Probability toolkit

  • Independent events: knowing one tells nothing about the other (drawing marbles with eyes closed).
  • Dependent events: linked (two classmates who always arrive together).
  • Binary event: P(red) = μ, so P(not red) = 1 − μ. All probabilities sum to 1.
  • For independent events, multiply probabilities. Two events together are less likely than either alone.
  • Possible vs probable: something may be possible but with near-zero probability.

Sample vs population

A sample may not represent the population. Two ways to get one: choose it deliberately (stratified, e.g. a statistics office sampling different groups) or choose completely at random. A non-random sample is biased. Complete randomness is not always possible, which is why one sample is not enough.

7. The coin experiment

Fair coin: P(heads) = ½. Ten heads in a row: (½)¹⁰ = 1/1024. Now give 1000 people a coin and ask each to flip 10 times.

  • P(a person does not get 10 heads) = 1023/1024.
  • P(nobody does) = (1023/1024)¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.377.
  • P(at least one does) = 1 − 0.377 ≈ 0.623.

A very unlikely event becomes fairly likely if you repeat the experiment enough. In learning, many samples or hypotheses can make a fluke look real.

8. The exponential function

For a line y = mx + b the growth rate (slope) is constant. For an exponential y = bˣ the growth rate changes: a small change in x can produce a big change in y.
  • Base > 1: y grows as x grows. Base < 1: y shrinks as x grows (decay).
  • The special base is e ≈ 2.718 (Euler’s number).
  • Applications named: expanding galaxies, chemical equations, anything with repeated processes.
  • e appears when something repeats many times: (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368 as n grows. That is why the “nobody got 10 heads” result is about 37%, and “at least one” about 63%.

Standard fact (not clearly stated in the transcript): the slope of eˣ equals eˣ itself.

Traps. (1) Independent events multiply, they do not add. (2) “Not at all” and “at least once” are complements. (3) eˣ does not have a constant slope. (4) Possible is not probable.

Summary

  • Supervised (labels), Unsupervised (clusters, centroid), Reinforcement (reward and penalty).
  • Population vs sample. We learn from a sample to infer the population.
  • H = hypothesis set, h = chosen one, f = unknown target, g = best approximation.
  • Feasibility of learning is answered with probability.
  • Independent → multiply. P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • 10 heads in a row = (½)¹⁰ = 1/1024. With 1000 tries, P(at least one) ≈ 0.62–0.63.
  • Exponential: changing slope, base e ≈ 2.718, limit (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e.
