Hoeffding and the feasibility of learning

Lecture 3 exam system, built from a rough speech-to-text transcript. Verify formulas and notation against your slides.

1. The problem we left open

We draw a line on the points we can see (say red and green) and it looks correct. But we never saw every point, so on new data the line may misclassify. We can never know the whole population, so we learn from a sample and ask how far the sample can mislead us.

  • ν (nu) is the quantity measured on the sample. μ (mu) is the same quantity on the population. The lecture says it will switch to different notation later, but they play the same roles.
  • We do not know μ. We only estimate it with ν.

2. What is a “bad event”?

Bad event = our learned rule predicts wrongly on a new data point because the sample misled us. We want the probability of a bad event to be as small as possible.

Intuition: the bigger the sample, the closer it is to the population, so the smaller the chance of a bad event. A bigger sample size N is the practical proxy for knowing more about the population.

3. Measuring quality with errors

Any candidate line (hypothesis) is judged by the errors it makes. We want the model with fewer errors.

  • In-sample error (Ein): error on the data we trained on, playing the role of ν.
  • Out-of-sample error (Eout): error on the population or new data, playing the role of μ.
  • For regression, errors are the vertical differences between line and points, summed.
  • Two different lines can be “the same” to us if they make the same errors.
Bad event: |Ein − Eout| > ε, where ε is a small tolerance we choose.

4. Hoeffding inequality

P( |ν − μ| > ε ) ≤ 2 · e−2ε²N
  • It gives an upper bound (“less than”), not an exact value. It is approximate and probable, so we speak of probability that can be measured, not of “possible”.
  • N up → exponent more negative → bound drops very fast.
  • ε down (stricter tolerance) → bound rises. If it exceeds 1 it tells us nothing.
  • It does not need the unknown μ; it depends on N and ε only.

Worked numbers: ε = 0.1, N = 100 gives 2e⁻² ≈ 0.27. N = 500 gives 2e⁻¹⁰ ≈ 9×10⁻⁵. N = 1000 gives 2e⁻²⁰ ≈ 4×10⁻⁹.

5. Many hypotheses: two factors

We usually choose among M hypotheses, and the final choice g (our approximation of the unknown target f) could be bad if any of them is bad. Adding the probabilities over hypotheses multiplies the bound by M (the lecture says “sum over the hypotheses”):

P( |Ein(g) − Eout(g)| > ε ) ≤ 2M · e−2ε²N

So two factors control generalization: the number of data points N (more is better) and the number of hypotheses M (more is riskier). They complement each other.

6. Testing, memorization and limits

  • Memorizing the training data is not learning. A test on data you trained on proves nothing, so hide some points and test on them (example in the lecture: 700 of 1000 for training, repeating with different selections).
  • Increasing data helps, but if you only memorize a pattern you will answer wrongly on unseen cases. The lecture warns against studying “two or three books” and thinking you know everything.
  • Learning stays a probability of being right or wrong. There is always a small chance of failure.

Properties: the lecture credits the idea to Hoeffding. The result makes learning probable (measurable) and approximate (we bound, we do not equate).

7. Study advice from the lecture

Review the same day, after two days, and after a week; the chance of forgetting drops (the lecturer quotes about 15%). Write it out and explain it to yourself or a peer. Understand the intuition and rebuild the formula instead of memorizing it.

Traps. (1) Hoeffding is “≤”, not “=”. (2) Bigger N lowers the bound, bigger M raises it. (3) Smaller ε raises the bound. (4) A bound above 1 is useless. (5) Ein is sample, Eout is population.

Memory trigger: “More Numbers, fewer Mistakes (N up, bound down).”

Summary

  • Sample (ν, Ein) vs population (μ, Eout). We never know μ.
  • Bad event: sample misleads, so |ν − μ| > ε.
  • Hoeffding: P ≤ 2e−2ε²N.
  • N ↑ → bound ↓ (exponentially). ε ↓ → bound ↑.
  • With M hypotheses: 2M·e−2ε²N.
  • Hypotheses are judged by their errors.
  • Test on unseen data. Memorizing is not learning.
  • Result is probable and approximate.
